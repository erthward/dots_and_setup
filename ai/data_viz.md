# general data viz principles:
- **down with chartjunk!**: a variable should never be shown in two ways at once (e.g., point size and color); a thing should never be colored for no clear reason
- **up with clear visual grammar!**: colormaps should match their variables (e.g., divergent if the variable of interest is centered on 0 or some other focal central value, continuous otherwise; perceptually uniform or nearly so is ideal; don't overplot too much data or make other mistakes that can create spurious apparent visual 'results')
- **colorblind-friendly**: as much as possible (but this sometimes can't be accommodated with my work, e.g., I have too many distinct categories for a discrete colormap; I'm mapping the results of a multivariate analysis in RGB space, as I have used for genetic PCA or for my seasonality maps)
- **less is more**: don't add unnecessary visual clutter (e.g., long labels that could be simpler symbols; grid lines that don't actually help discern differences in plotted values)
- **color is a guide**: as much as possible, use a fixed set of colors to help 'walk the eye' across multiple panels in a plot or multiple separate but related plots (e.g. blue and red refer to winter and summer in a consistent way across all the plots in a study)
- **look for the 'one killer figure'**: a study can really be pinned down in a paper and in readers' memories if it has the one killer figure that grounds it. This can be a main figure with top-line results. It can also be a foundational conceptual figure that the rest of the study is based on and with an aesthetic language that the rest of the empirical results' figures match up with.
- **videos are underrated!**: I created a single video for my Nature paper (the whole globe, with each pixel's long-term average phenology according to remote sensing, self-normalized to emphasize spatial differences in timing regardless of spatial differences in amplitude). It was one of the best decisions in that whole study! It was not only an exploratory analysis step that created a 'lava lamp' that gave me a ton of intuitive understanding, but it also got a lot of interest and mileage in talks, news interviews, etc. Journals will also accept supplementary videos. Because I typically study spatiotemporal complexity in ecology, evolution, and environment contexts, videos can often convey a combined sense of time and space that static summary figures just do not. Don't overlook this opportunity!
